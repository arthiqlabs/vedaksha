\begin{abstract}
Vedaksha is a clean-room ephemeris and Vedic astrology (jyotish) computation engine written
in Rust. It computes planetary positions from the analytical VSOP87A and ELP/MPP02 series
and from the JPL DE440s numerical kernel. Re-measured on 2026-10-24 against NASA/JPL
Horizons DE441, the numerical path has a mean apparent geocentric ecliptic-longitude
residual of 0.096 arcseconds and a maximum of 0.272 arcseconds over
15,350 comparisons spanning 1900--2025. The analytical path has a mean of 0.160 arcseconds
and a maximum of 1.473 arcseconds over 13,815 comparisons in the same interval.
The sub-arcsecond description refers to mean longitude agreement, not a bound on every
analytical result or on other coordinates. From 1962-01-20, the oracle converts Horizons'
UTC time tags to Terrestrial Time using the historical TAI minus UTC record, removing
both $\Delta T$ and UT1 minus UTC from that part of the comparison.
Version 10 corrects the nutation coefficients and the VSOP87A frame rotation, includes
solar gravitational light deflection, and updates $\Delta T$ from measured and predicted
records. The engine exposes derived jyotish computations through native Rust, Python,
WebAssembly, and Model Context Protocol interfaces. Primary sources and derivation records
are documented in a public provenance ledger. An external parity harness measures the
analytical path against JPL DE440 and IMCCE INPOP21a and over six centuries either side
of J2000.0.
The software is source-available under BUSL-1.1, with conversion to Apache-2.0 four years
after each version's release.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

An ephemeris result is defined by more than a planetary theory: its time scale, target,
reference frame, and apparent-place corrections determine what a longitude means.
Vedaksha makes these choices explicit and records the sources of its algorithms and data.
ArthIQ Labs, an AI platforms company, develops the engine for computational jyotish
and machine-accessible astronomical calculations.

Here ``clean-room'' describes a source and derivation procedure. The analytical theories
come from their authors' publications and coefficient distributions; the numerical kernel
comes from NASA/JPL. The repository's \texttt{DATA\_PROVENANCE.md} records source URLs,
retrieval dates, licensing, and hashes where established. Dated records under
\texttt{docs/audit/} document the lunar-theory and ayanamsha derivations, including the
replacement of early sidereal-offset constants by primary-source derivations.
Those records support a procedural provenance claim; numerical agreement alone cannot
establish one.

This report treats the ephemeris and sidereal-conversion methods in detail. The derived
jyotish layer, including nakshatras, dashas, vargas, panchanga, shadbala, and muhurta, is
described at the interface level. Its outputs do not inherit an accuracy guarantee merely
because their input longitudes have been tested.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Analytical ephemeris pipeline}

VSOP87A supplies planetary positions from Mercury through Neptune
\citep{bretagnon1988vsop87}. ELP/MPP02 supplies lunar positions
\citep{chapront2003elpmpp02}.

The coefficient tree contains 331 files at this checkout, counted in the ephemeris
crate's \path{src/analytical/coefficients/} directory.
Generated binary tables are embedded in the program, so this path requires no runtime
data file. Lunar phases are evaluated by angle addition through a shared prefix tree;
VSOP87A reuses sine and cosine evaluations across terms with the same frequency.
No performance benchmark was re-run for this report.

VSOP87A vectors are transformed from the dynamical ecliptic and equinox of J2000 to FK5
J2000 by the matrix published with the series in Bretagnon and Francou's
\texttt{vsop87.doc}, ``REFERENCE SYSTEM'' \citep{bretagnon1988vsop87}.
The implementation's \texttt{VSOP87A\_TO\_EQUATORIAL} applies this matrix to both position
and velocity. Its elements were checked against the authors' distribution for this revision.
This replaces the former rotation by the IAU 1976 obliquity alone, which omitted the
published equinox rotation. The matrix targets FK5; the implementation applies no additional
FK5-to-ICRS transformation. The Moon still uses the IAU 1976 J2000 obliquity,
84381.448 arcseconds. Its frame relation was not independently re-derived for this report,
so the former claim that this rotation was verified for both theories is withdrawn.

\subsection{Numerical ephemeris pipeline}

The second implementation behind \texttt{EphemerisProvider} reads the DE440s SPK kernel
from NAIF \citep{naif2021de440s,park2021de440}.
Vedaksha's DAF/SPK parser evaluates type-2 Chebyshev position segments and rejects requests
outside the kernel's segment coverage. The kernel used here was downloaded and checked
against the repository's pinned SHA256 before measurement.
The comparison targets planetary-system barycentres, matching the segments DE440s stores,
with the Sun and Moon addressed separately. A comparison against physical planet centres
would measure a different quantity.

Horizons is an external validation service, not a kernel ingested by Vedaksha.
Its DE441 reference is distinct from DE440s, but these are not independent observational
lineages: DE440 and DE441 were fitted to the same data, with a different lunar-core damping
assumption \citep{park2021de440}. Agreement tests the implementation against an external
result without proving independence of all underlying models or observations.

\subsection{Apparent place and reference frames}

The apparent-place pipeline applies light-time and aberration corrections and, for planets,
solar gravitational light deflection. The latter uses the finite-distance weak-field
expression of \citet[eqs.~75 and 77]{klioner2003deflection}.
\texttt{deflection::solar\_deflection} operates on the astrometric source vector and the
observer's displacement from the Sun. The Sun and Moon are excluded. For a straight ray
crossing the solar disc, the implementation returns zero displacement as an occultation
convention; this is not a physical prediction of an observable undeflected ray.
Deflection by other bodies and solar multipole terms are not modelled.

The frame of date uses IAU 2006 precession in the four-angle Fukushima and Williams
parameterization \citep{capitaine2003precession}, composed as
$R_x(-\epsilon_A)R_z(-\bar\psi)R_x(\bar\phi)R_z(\bar\gamma)$.
Nutation is then applied before rotation to the ecliptic of date.
The nutation table contains the first 77 luni-solar rows of IAU 2000A, the truncated
series used for IAU 2000B \citep{mccarthy2003nutation}.
A row-by-row comparison with the IAU 2000A coefficients represented in ERFA confirms all
77 current rows. Relative to the preceding implementation, 30 rows required correction;
17 had the main longitude coefficient's sign reversed. Thus the previous report's
unqualified description of the old table as the published series was incorrect.
The implementation omits the full planetary terms, the fixed planetary-bias offsets of
2000B, and free core nutation. It should therefore be read as the corrected truncated
luni-solar implementation, not as a claim to implement every part of the full IAU model.

$\Delta T=\mathrm{TT}-\mathrm{UT1}$. The TT-addressed
\texttt{ecliptic\_position\_tt} entry point bypasses that conversion.
At v10.0.0, \texttt{delta\_t.rs} uses the following sources.

\subsection{Sidereal (ayanamsha) conversion}

Sidereal longitude subtracts the true ayanamsha from apparent tropical longitude.
The true ayanamsha is the mean precession-only offset plus nutation in longitude;
using the mean offset alone would retain a nutation term in the sidereal result.
Both forms have explicitly named APIs.

The engine implements eleven sidereal systems, in addition to a tropical option.
The ayanamsha audit dated 2026-08-17 records their normative definitions and
declared assumptions in \texttt{spec.md} and \texttt{derivation-inputs.json}.
These include the Indian official ayanamsha from
\emph{The Indian Astronomical Ephemeris}, the Fagan and Bradley, Krishnamurti, and Raman
book anchors, the \emph{Surya Siddhanta} (Ch.~3, vv.~9--12), Yukteshwar,
and the Revati, True Chitra, Pushya, True Mula, and Galactic Centre systems.
Star anchors use the Hipparcos re-reduction \citep{vanleeuwen2007hipparcos};
the Galactic Centre anchor uses the VLBI determination of Sagittarius A*
\citep{gordon2023sgra}.
The Rust implementation is checked against a separately written Python derivation from
the same input manifest. These share primary inputs and authorship, so agreement does
not exclude a shared transcription error. The measured fixture result is reported below.

\subsection{Provenance and verification boundary}

The committed source ledger and dated derivation records identify the inputs consulted.
For this revision, the nutation rows and VSOP87A matrix were checked against their
reference distributions, and the DE440s and committed Horizons fixture hashes were
verified. The lunar and planetary coefficient distributions were not regenerated,
the historical clean-room procedures were not repeated, and the Horizons fixture was
not fetched afresh. The longitude results below are new evaluations against the
existing pinned fixture.

\section{Validation}

\subsection{Oracle construction and time-tag matching}

The fixture \texttt{tests/oracle\_jpl/reference\_positions.json} contains 24,350 rows:
ten targets at 2,435 dates on a 30-day grid in 1900--2100, originally fetched on
2026-07-16 from Horizons \citep{giorgini2015horizons}.
The targets are the Sun, Moon, and planetary-system barycentres from Mercury through
Pluto. The tested quantity is the absolute shortest-arc difference in apparent
geocentric ecliptic longitude of date.
The analytical provider has no Pluto model, so its oracle evaluates 21,915 rows.

Horizons' ``UT'' tag means UT1 before 1962-01-20 and UTC from that date.
For the earlier rows, both tests call the UT1-addressed engine API, so the engine's
$\Delta T$ remains in the residual. For later rows they use
\[
 \mathrm{JD}_{\rm TT}=\mathrm{JD}_{\rm UTC}
       +\frac{32.184+(\mathrm{TAI}-\mathrm{UTC})}{86400},
\]
with offsets in seconds, then call \texttt{ecliptic\_position\_tt}.
The USNO historical TAI minus UTC table includes the pre-1972 rate terms as well as
subsequent leap seconds. The last known correction is retained for future dates,
matching Horizons' convention. This removes both $\Delta T$ and UT1 minus UTC from
the comparison from 1962-01-20 onward. It does not validate the engine's future
UT1-to-TT predictions or predict future leap seconds.

The 1900--2025 reporting window is retained to preserve the published metric's
definition and comparison count. It is now a mixture of two time-tag regimes,
not a boundary separating all measured time conversions from all predicted ones.
Horizons also uses an older precession/nutation convention for its ecliptic of date
than Vedaksha's IAU 2006/2000B pipeline. The residuals include this convention
difference; they are not isolated measurements of theory truncation.

experiment but does not eliminate differences in apparent-place conventions.

\subsection{Scope and other checks}

The longitude oracle does not measure latitude, distance, speed, lunar-node longitude,
house cusps, or derived jyotish events. A planetary-system barycentre is not a lunar
node: the latter requires a geometric or secular definition in addition to an ephemeris.
Node accuracy and secular drift were not measured for this release, so no
sub-arcsecond node claim is made. The supported API range is also not a measured
accuracy range; beyond the fixture dates, the only evidence is the sparse
full-range sample in Table~\ref{tab:fullrange}.

The ayanamsha fixture test made 192 comparisons against the committed Python-derived
reference and measured a worst disagreement of $5.684\times10^{-14}$ degrees
(Revati-paksha at year 285 CE). Its separate coverage check also passed. This is
agreement between two evaluations of shared primary inputs, not an external
accuracy bound on the sidereal definitions.
The three tests in \texttt{oracle\_time\_scale.rs} also passed, checking historical
TAI minus UTC values, the time-tag boundary, and the conversion against committed
Horizons time-offset samples.

The release's \texttt{metrics.json} records 1,603 per-push tests and 1,614 full-suite
tests, measured at tag v10.0.0. These are tag measurements, not counts from this
report's targeted runs. The full workspace suite was not re-run.

\section{Conclusion}

The v10.0.0 numerical path agrees with the pinned Horizons longitude reference to
0.096 arcseconds mean and 0.272 arcseconds maximum over 1900--2025;
the analytical path measures 0.160 arcseconds mean and 1.473 arcseconds maximum.
From 1962-01-20 the comparison uses the same TT instants on both sides, so
$\Delta T$ does not enter it. These results exercise the corrected nutation
table, published VSOP87A rotation, and solar-deflection path, while preserving
explicit limits on what was measured. Against two national-agency ephemerides the
analytical path measures 0.030 and 0.025 arcseconds mean, 0.696 arcseconds maximum, over
seven bodies. Node and house-cusp accuracy claims await the corresponding evidence.
